# Commentary on Hexbound

When I was ten or eleven years old, I had a friend who lived a few cities over. Whenever we wanted to visit, our parents would take us to a bookstore around halfway between our houses for "the great transfer". During one of these transfers, I got my hands on a copy of the *Dungeons & Dragons 4th Edition Player's Handbook* from either Borders (now bankrupt) or Barnes and Noble - I can't remember which, but I always personally preferred the Borders atmosphere.

I spent hours reading through all of the abilities, class descriptions, different races, and dreamt about magical items and weapons that only a Dungeon Master could understand. Not once did we play a real game, let alone a campaign, but I continued to feel the weight of the theoretical fun we could have had. I did eventually get into D&D 3.5 in high school, and then 5th edition in college, but the "MMO" style of 4th edition still haunted me. Now, twenty years later and with the magic of AI at my fingertips, I was finally able to put those ghosts to rest in the form of Hexbound.

Hexbound is everything I thought it would be like to play *Dungeons & Dragons 4th Edition*. As it turns out, playing solo gets old quickly, so I tried to spice it up with a Slay the Spire-esque campaign map.

As with all of my games, this one was implemented entirely by Claude, but this was the first game where I realized Claude can't do everything. The current implementation is about the limit of what I would be happy with without making some major changes by hand. The equipment system is not very satisfying, the campaign map is pretty barebones, and the tactics themselves get stale quickly.

Still, the final product feels like a convincing prototype of what a hex grid Spire-based tactics roguelite could be. This is one that I'd like to come back to and try to figure out what a real polished final product would look like.
